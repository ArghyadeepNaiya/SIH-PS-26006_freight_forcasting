Smart India Hackathon problem statement 26006. Ministry of Steel. Steel Authority of India Limited.

Freight Charter Decision Console

This system plans the sea journey that brings bulk raw material, such as coking coal or iron ore, from another country to a steel plant in eastern India. You say what you need and when you need it. The system chooses what size of ship to hire and which Indian port it should unload at. It then tells you what one tonne will have cost by the time it reaches the plant gate, and whether to book a ship today or wait.

Shipping has a lot of trade words in it. Each one is marked with a dotted underline the first time it appears on a screen. Rest the mouse pointer on a marked word and its meaning appears. Every one of them is also written out in ordinary English on the plain word list screen, which is the last of the five listed below, so nothing here depends on being able to use a mouse.

What this screen does, in plain words

You need to buy raw material from overseas and land it at an Indian steel plant. Before anyone can buy it, somebody has to hire a ship to carry it. This screen answers the three questions that go with that decision.

  1. What size of ship should be hired for this load.
  2. Which port on the east coast of India it should unload at.
  3. What one tonne will cost by the time it reaches the plant gate, and whether it is better to book the ship today or to wait.

Fill in the five boxes on the left and then choose the button marked get recommendation. The system compares every size of ship against every port, removes the combinations that physically cannot work, and puts what is left in order of cost. It also lists the ones it removed and says which rule removed each of them.

Nothing is ordered and no message is sent to anybody. This screen only produces advice that you read here.

What this screen does, in plain words

The price of hiring a ship moves every day, in much the same way the price of an air ticket does. This screen shows what that price has done over the last two years for each size of ship, and what our computer model expects it to do next.

It also does something that most tools quietly avoid. It tells you whether our own forecast is worth listening to at all. It does that by testing our model against the laziest forecast anyone could make, which is to say that tomorrow's price will be whatever today's price is. A forecast that cannot beat that lazy guess is not adding anything, and when ours cannot beat it we say so on this screen instead of hiding it.

Can this forecast be trusted

We measure that with one number, which forecasters call a skill score. It compares the size of our model's mistakes against the size of the mistakes made by simply assuming that the price does not change at all.

  1. Above zero means our model made smaller mistakes than the lazy guess, so it is worth listening to.
  2. Zero or below means it did not, so you should ignore the forecast and treat today's price as the best information you have.

The list below reports that test for each size of ship and for each length of time ahead. Where the answer is unfavourable to us, it still says so.

    When to go into the market

    Three separate questions, kept separate on purpose. Whether our model can see anything. What the calendar does, if anything. And how unstable prices are right now. A seasonal pattern is not evidence that our model works, and a working model is not evidence of a seasonal pattern, so merging them into one score would hide the very distinction that makes this system trustworthy.

    What this screen does, in plain words

    A port cannot accept every ship that turns up. The water in front of it has a certain depth, the berth has a certain length, and a loaded ship that sits deeper in the water than the port is deep cannot get in. This screen lists those physical limits for every east coast port the system considers.

    This is why the biggest ship is often the wrong answer. A big ship is cheaper for each tonne only if you can fill it. At a shallow port it cannot be filled, and a half empty voyage costs very nearly the same as a full one, so the cost of each tonne actually delivered goes up rather than down.

    When the recommendation screen refuses a ship, the limit that refused it is on this screen.

    Every number below says where it came from. A number marked assumption is a placeholder we chose ourselves. It has not been confirmed with the port or against a published document, so it must be checked with the port authority before anyone uses this system for a real purchase.

    What this screen does, in plain words

    The screen before this one lists what a port can take in principle. This screen shows what is actually going on at each port at the moment, and it comes from two places.

    1. The port's own staff sign in to a separate password protected page and state how much yard space they have free, which sizes of ship they can take, how long ships are waiting now, and which materials they are short of. Everything they state is stamped with their name and the date they stated it.
    2. The weather forecast for the next several days at each dock and at each waiting area out at sea. Bad weather stops unloading, and every day lost that way is charged to you, so it is priced into the recommendations on the first screen.

    What a port operator states here is their own claim about their own port. It is not a published figure from the port authority, and this screen never pretends otherwise.

      What this screen does, in plain words

      This is the screen the whole project exists for. The first screen prices one shipload. This one plans a whole year, or half of one, and answers the question the first screen cannot. Should you keep hiring a ship every time you need one, at whatever the price happens to be that day, or should you sign one contract now that covers all the voyages at a rate agreed in advance.

      1. Buying each voyage as you need it is called buying on the spot market. You pay whatever the price is on the day. Sometimes that is cheap and sometimes it is not, and you find out which only after you have committed.
      2. Signing one contract for many voyages is called a term contract. You know the price for the whole period on the day you sign. The owner will usually want a little extra for taking that risk off you.
      3. You do not have to choose one or the other. Covering part of the programme on a term contract and buying the rest on the spot market is normally the right answer, and this screen works out what that share should be.

      The comparison uses no forecast at all, which matters, because on the data loaded today our forecast is not reliable. Instead it measures what the spot market has actually done over every period of this length in its whole history, and asks whether the rate on offer beats that record.

      Your buying programme

      What you need over the coming months, not what you need in one ship.

      The whole quantity for the period, across every shipload. Six hundred thousand tonnes is roughly nine Panamax loads.

      If an owner has quoted you a rate, put it here as a percentage of today's spot rate. 105 means five percent above today. 95 means five percent below. Leave it empty and the comparison assumes an offer at exactly today's rate, and tells you the highest rate that would still be worth signing.

      This does not send anything to anybody. It prices your programme three ways and shows the three side by side.

      What this screen does, in plain words

      The same material can be bought from several countries, and the first screen assumes you have already chosen one. This screen questions that choice. It prices the same quantity of the same material landed at the same plant, from every country in this system that can supply it, and puts them in order of what a tonne actually costs at the plant gate.

      The answer is often not the nearest country. A short voyage from a shallow loading port can be dearer than a long voyage from a deep one, because the shallow port cannot fill the ship, and a half empty ship costs almost as much to sail as a full one. That is the effect this screen exists to make visible.

      This uses the material, quantity and destination plant you chose on the first screen. Change them there and run this again.

      What this screen does, in plain words

      Everything else on this system tells you what a tonne costs. This screen tells you what could go wrong with that number. Five things are watched all the time, and anything that looks unusual is written up here with the evidence that raised it and what you can do about it.

      1. The freight market, watched for prices swinging more violently than usual, which is when a fixed rate is worth the most.
      2. Our own forecast, which is flagged whenever it fails its reliability test, so that nobody reads it as advice when it is not.
      3. The ports, by comparing the queue a port operator reports today against the waiting time normally published for that port.
      4. The weather at each port over the coming week.
      5. The data itself, because a confident looking number built on unverified placeholders is a risk even when the arithmetic is perfect.

      Warnings are sorted with the most serious first. Every one of them names where the evidence came from, so none of them has to be taken on trust.
